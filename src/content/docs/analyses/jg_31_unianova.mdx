---
title: "ANOVA: One-Way"
---

Use a one-way ANOVA to compare the means of a continuous outcome across three or more independent groups.

:::note[Follow-along example]
- Download the starting dataset and follow along: [Exam_Scores.csv](/output/Exam_Scores.csv)
- Download the completed jamovi file to see each output already created: [Exam_Scores_One_Way_ANOVA.omv](/output/Exam_Scores_One_Way_ANOVA.omv)
:::

In this example, students were randomly assigned to take their final exam on white, blue, or red paper.
The study tested whether exam paper colour affected students' final exam scores (out of 75).
Use a one-way ANOVA to compare mean exam scores across these three independent groups.

Follow the steps below to run and interpret this example in jamovi.

## Run the analysis

1.  Open `Exam_Scores.csv` in jamovi and check that the variables are set up correctly.

    You need:

    - one <img src="/images/variable-continuous.svg" alt="continuous measure icon" style="width: 16px" /> continuous dependent variable: `Exam_Score`
    - one <img src="/images/variable-nominal.svg" alt="nominal measure icon" style="width: 16px" /> grouping variable with three or more levels: `Colour`

    Your data should be arranged like this:

    <img src="/images/jg_data_format_unianova.jpg" alt="Example data layout for a one-way ANOVA" style="width: 100.0%" />

    :::note
    Only `Exam_Score` and `Colour` are needed for this analysis. The other columns describe additional aspects of the experiment.

    For related analyses, see:

    - [Factorial ANOVA](/analyses/jg_32_anova-factorial) to examine two factors, such as paper colour and lighting conditions, and their interaction.
    - [Repeated-measures ANOVA](/analyses/jg_33_anova-rm) to compare related measurements from the same students, such as scores on different exam components.
    - [Mixed ANOVA](/analyses/jg_34_anova-mixed) to compare related measurements across independent groups.
    :::

2.  Select `Analyses` → `ANOVA` → `One-Way ANOVA`.

    <img src="/images/jg_select_unianova.jpg" alt="Opening One-Way ANOVA from the Analyses menu" style="width: 45.0%" />

3.  Drag `Exam_Score` to the `Dependent Variable` box and `Colour` to the `Grouping Variable` box.

    <img src="/images/jg_add_var_unianova.jpg" alt="Assigning variables for the one-way ANOVA" style="width: 70.0%" />

4.  Select the options you need.

    Under `Variances`, switch from the default, `Don't assume equal (Welch's)`, to `Assume equal (Fisher's)` for this example.
    Fisher's ANOVA assumes equal variances across groups.

    Under `Assumption Checks`, select `Normality test` and `Homogeneity test`.

    Under `Additional Statistics`, select `Descriptives table` and `Descriptives plot` to show the group means and a plot.

    <img src="/images/jg_options_unianova.jpg" alt="One-way ANOVA options in jamovi" style="width: 70.0%" />

    Because the ANOVA is significant, use post hoc tests to find which groups differ.
    Open `Post-Hoc Tests` and select `Tukey`.

    <img src="/images/jg_post_hoc_options_unianova.jpg" alt="One-way ANOVA post-hoc test options in jamovi" style="width: 70.0%" />

    :::note
    The default, `Don't assume equal (Welch's)`, runs Welch's ANOVA, which does not require equal variances across groups. Use this option if the equal-variances assumption is not met.
    :::

5.  Read the output.

    Start with the descriptives table and plot. The Blue group had the highest mean exam score, followed by the White group, then the Red group.

    <img src="/images/jg_descriptives_table_unianova.jpg" alt="Descriptive statistics table of exam scores by paper colour" style="width: 70.0%" />

    <img src="/images/jg_descriptives_plot_unianova.jpg" alt="Descriptive statistics plot of exam scores by paper colour" style="width: 70.0%" />

    In the `One-Way ANOVA` table, check the `p` value. Here, `p < .001`, indicating that at least one group's mean exam score differs from the others.

    <img src="/images/jg_output_unianova.jpg" alt="One-way ANOVA output table" style="width: 70.0%" />

    Use the `Tukey Post-Hoc Test - Exam_Score` table to identify which groups differ.

    <img src="/images/jg_post_hoc_unianova.jpg" alt="Tukey post hoc comparisons of exam scores between paper colour groups" style="width: 70.0%" />

    The Blue and White groups did not differ significantly (`p = .453`). The Red group scored significantly lower than both the Blue group (`p < .001`) and the White group (`p = .001`).

6.  Check the assumptions.

    In the `Normality Test (Shapiro–Wilk)` table, a `p` value above `.05` means there is no evidence of non-normality. Here, `p = .160`.

    In the `Homogeneity of Variances Test (Levene's)` table, a `p` value above `.05` means there is no evidence of unequal variances. Here, `p = .131`.

    <img src="/images/jg_assumptions_unianova.jpg" alt="Shapiro-Wilk normality and Levene's homogeneity of variance test tables" style="width: 70.0%" />

## Write up the Analysis

    A one-way ANOVA tested whether mean exam scores differed across the white, blue, and red paper groups, *F*(2, 27) = 14.6, *p* < .001.
    Tukey-adjusted comparisons showed that the Red group (*M* = 35.5, *SD* = 9.07) scored lower than the Blue group (*M* = 52.5, *SD* = 6.04), *p* < .001, and the White group (*M* = 48.5, *SD* = 6.57), *p* = .001. The Blue and White groups did not differ significantly, *p* = .453.
    Assumption checks showed no evidence of non-normality or unequal variances.

    :::note
    This is a common APA 7-style write-up. Check your discipline's or journal's style guide for additional reporting requirements.
    :::
